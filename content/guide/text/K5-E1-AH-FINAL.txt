         SHE BLINDED ME WITH SCIENCE

  The world of color is a fascinating one, originating from the vast electromagnetic
  spectrum…that stretches infinitely (whoa!).

  But don’t worry, I’m not going to make you break out the science books and study
  Issac Newton to understand it! However, there are a couple of important things we
  should note about the physiological aspects of how we perceive color.

  What we see as color is just a tiny sliver of this spectrum, a fragment of visible light.
  The cool thing is, these colors aren’t random; they emerge from specific wavelengths
  and frequencies that weave their way through the Universe, creating the vibrant
  palette we perceive with our eyes.

  Our eyes rely on specialized cone cells to detect various wavelengths of light, allowing
  us to perceive different colors. These cone cells are sensitive to specific ranges,
  roughly corresponding to blue, green, and red. When these cones are stimulated
  in varying proportions, our brain interprets the mix as a particular color, forming the
  basis for our color vision. However, not everyone perceives colors in the same way, as
  conditions like color blindness can affect one’s ability to distinguish between certain
  hues.

                     WHEEL OF FORTUNE

   So what’s a color wheel anyway?

   A color wheel is a visual tool that helps us to understand the relationships between
   colors and how they interact with one another.

   It’s a visual roadmap that simplifies the complexities of color, allowing artists,
   designers, and anyone interested in working with color to make informed choices
   about different combinations, contrast, and harmony.

   To get you immersed in the world of color, take a look at the key components and
   concepts of the color wheel below. Equipped with this foundational knowledge,
   you’ll be able to use the color wheel with confidence and intention:




ABUNDANT HOME
                                COLOR - BROAD STROKES




   1.Primary Colors: At the core of the color wheel are three primary colors: red,
   blue, and yellow. These colors are considered fundamental because they cannot
   be created by mixing other colors together. Instead, all other colors are derived
   from various combinations of these primaries.

   2. Secondary Colors: Between the primary colors on the color wheel, you’ll
   find three secondary colors: orange (created by mixing red and yellow), green
   (from yellow and blue), and purple (a result of combining blue and red). These
   secondary colors are made by mixing equal parts of two adjacent primary colors.

   3. Tertiary Colors: Tertiary colors are located between primary and secondary
   colors on the wheel. They are created by mixing a primary color with an adjacent
   secondary color. Examples include red-orange, yellow-green, and blue-purple.
   Tertiary colors offer more subtle and complex shades.

   4. Color Relationships: The color wheel is a valuable tool for understanding
   color relationships and harmonies. Various color schemes, such as monochromatic
   (using shades and tints of a single hue), triadic (using three evenly spaced colors),
   and split-complementary (using a base color and the two colors adjacent to its
   complement), can be created using the wheel.

   5. Complementary Colors: Complementary colors are positioned directly
   opposite each other on the color wheel. They create a strong contrast when
   placed side by side and tend to enhance each other’s intensity. Examples of
   complementary pairs include red and green, blue and orange, and yellow and
   purple.

   6. Analogous Colors: You can think of analogous colors as neighbors because
   they are situated next to each other on the color wheel. They share a common
   primary color and can create a harmonious and pleasing color scheme. For
   instance, yellow, yellow-green, and green form an analogous color scheme.

   7. Triadic: Triadic colors are three evenly spaced colors on the color wheel. If
   you looked at a color wheel and connected these three colors together with
   lines, they would form an equilateral triangle. Red, yellow and blue are a triad,
   as well as green, orange, purple. A triadic color scheme can act similarly as a
   complementary one and can add vibrancy and balance.


   8. Warm and Cool Colors: The color wheel also allows us to distinguish between
   warm and cool colors. Warm colors, such as reds, oranges, and yellows, convey
   energy and warmth. In contrast, cool colors like blues and greens evoke a sense
   of calm and relaxation.




   Rarely would you use the exact colors portrayed in a basic color wheel! They
   are very vibrant and bold. The color wheel is just a guide; the deliciousness and
   beauty of the full-color spectrum come in the fine-tuning of the colors to align
   with your specific intentions and desires.

                      SOME LIKE IT HOT




   The color wheel is divided into two distinct worlds:

   On one side, you have warm colors, consisting of fiery reds, and sunny yellows. These
   colors seem to leap off the canvas, advancing towards you. They’re like a welcoming
   invitation, a burst of stimulation for your senses.

   On the other side, there’s the realm of cool colors, from serene blue-greens to tranquil
   blue-violets. These colors, like a calm overcast day or the coolness of winter, seem to
   recede into the background. They tend to exude a sense of calmness and relaxation.

   But just like everything, it’s all relative. Think of it as a color’s social circle. Whether a color
   feels warm or cool isn’t set in stone; it’s all about the company it keeps. The perception of
   warmth or coolness can shift depending on the colors it’s surrounded by on the wheel.

   Since we are talking about color temperature, this is the perfect time to dive into how
   colors actually make us feel.




   Warm Colors:
   Warm colors are found on one side of the color wheel and include shades like red, orange,
   and yellow. Here are some characteristics and associations of warm colors:

   • Emotional Impact: Warm colors are often associated with energy, warmth, and
   passion. They can evoke strong emotions and create a sense of excitement and intensity.
   Think of the fiery red of a sunset or the vibrant orange of a blazing fire.
   • Visual Effects: Warm colors tend to advance or appear closer to the viewer in an
   artwork or design. They can create a feeling of coziness and intimacy when used in
   interiors.
   • Use in Design: Designers often use warm colors to draw attention, convey enthusiasm,
   and stimulate the senses. They can be effective in areas where you want to encourage
   social interaction and energy, such as dining rooms or entertainment spaces.

   Cool Colors:
   Cool colors are located on the opposite side of the color wheel and include shades like
   blue, green, and purple. Here are some characteristics and associations of cool colors:

   • Emotional Impact: Cool colors are typically associated with calmness, serenity, and
   relaxation. They can create a soothing and tranquil atmosphere, like the calming blue of
   a clear sky or the refreshing green of a lush forest.
   • Visual Effects: Cool colors tend to recede or appear more distant in an artwork or
   design. They can create a feeling of spaciousness and openness when used in interiors.
   • Use in Design: Designers often use cool colors in spaces where they want to promote
   relaxation, concentration, or a sense of coolness. Cool colors are commonly seen in
   bedrooms, bathrooms, and workspaces.

              COLOR FUNDAMENTALS
   Before we delve into how you can use color in your space, let’s talk about some
   fundamental elements of color.

   Each of the following elements plays a unique role in shaping how we perceive and
   interact with the vibrant spectrum that surrounds us. From the pure essence of hue
   to the subtle interplay of shade and tint, and the nuanced dimensions of value and
   saturation, these elements form the building blocks of color theory.




     turbulences-deco.fr


   Hue:
   • Hue is the pure, fundamental color itself, such as red, blue, or yellow.
   • It is what we commonly think of as the color’s name.
   • The color wheel is organized based on different hues.

   Value:
   • Value refers to the lightness or darkness of a color.
   • It is determined by the amount of black or white added to the hue.
   • Value is pivotal in creating contrast and depth in design.


ABUNDANT HOME
                                 COLOR - BROAD STROKES




  homeadore.com


  Saturation (Chroma):
  • Saturation, also known as chroma, refers to the color’s intensity and represents the purity
  or vividness of a color.
  • Highly saturated colors are vibrant and intense, while desaturated colors are more muted
  or pastel-like.
  • Saturation plays a role in the emotional impact of a color.

  Shade:
  • A shade is a variation of a color that is created by adding black to the original hue.
  • It results in a darker and often more subdued version of the color.
  • Shades are useful for creating contrast, depth, and a sense of seriousness in design.

  Tint:
  • A tint is a variation of a color that is created by adding white to the original hue.
  • It results in a lighter and softer version of the color.
  • Tints convey feelings of lightness, airiness, and purity and are often used to establish a
  cheerful or inviting atmosphere.

  Tone:
  • Tone is a broader concept that encompasses both the lightness and darkness of a color,
  referring to its overall brightness.
  • It is influenced by the addition of gray (black and white) to the original hue.
  • Tone is essential in achieving balance and harmony in color schemes.

              BEYOND THE WHEEL
  Black, white, gray, and brown are typically treated as neutral colors in color theory
  and are not included on the color wheel. But they are all versatile and valuable tools
  for achieving balance, contrast, and subtlety in color schemes. Whether you want a
  cool and modern look with gray or a warm and natural ambiance with brown, these
  neutrals play a pivotal role in creating balanced and harmonious color compositions.

  Interestingly enough, there is a bit of nuance to these neutrals- particularly when we
  focus on black and white.

  In the world of color, the question of whether black and white should be classified as
  colors is a subject of ongoing debate in the world of color. And depending on who
  you ask or which theory you are discussing, you will receive varying answers that
  reflect different perspectives.

  Consider Color Harmony: Just as lead singers add supporting backup vocals to

  beige, or white to provide a sense of balance and prevent the space from feeling
  overwhelming.

  To make sure your palette is cohesive and balanced, you can utilize the concepts
  that you learned about the color wheel. For instance, if you are looking for prosperity

  reds and greens throughout your space, as they are on opposite sides of the color

  growth, an analogous scheme might better suit your needs.

  You can also take another approach. If you have multiple desires (don’t we all?),
